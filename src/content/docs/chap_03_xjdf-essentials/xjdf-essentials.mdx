The Exchange Job Definition Format (XJDF) is designed as a pure data interchange format. XJDF Documents are generated at runtime to exchange information between software applications and production devices, with each document containing only the data tailored specifically to its recipient.

Technically, XJDF defines two related but distinct perspectives on a print job: the **product view** and the **process view**. The product view is a process-independent product description that “describes the final product from the perspective of the print buyer. The customer or product designer will typically describe the desired final product without any knowledge of the manufacturing process” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 2.2.1 "Product Intent and Processes"]. It specifies characteristics such as the finished dimensions of the product, paper quality, and folding requirements, without concern for production details like press sheet dimensions or imposition layouts. All manufacturing-specific instructions are defined in the process view of the XJDF Document, which describes how individual workflow steps are to be executed, on which device, and when. The following figure illustrates how both perspectives are represented in an XJDF Document:

  <Image src={productVsProcessView} alt="Product view vs. process view" />
  <figcaption>Figure: Product view vs. process view in XJDF</figcaption>
</figure>

The product view is built around `Product` elements containing one or more `Intent` elements, such as `FoldingIntent`, `LayoutIntent`, and `BindingIntent`. Each `Intent` element acts as a container for a specific category of product characteristics—for example, `FoldingIntent` captures all folding details of the desired product. The XJDF Specification provides a rich set of intent types capable of describing a wide variety of print products.

In addition to the product description, XJDF defines the production steps executed in a printing house to manufacture the final product. **In XJDF, individual production steps are called processes.** A production workflow is a sequence of processes, where each process defined in the XJDF Specification is “assumed to be executed by a single-purpose device” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 5.1 "Combining Individual Process Steps"]. Examples include `ConventionalPrinting`, `DigitalPrinting`, `Cutting`, and `Folding`. Each process in the specification is defined by a unique name, a semantic description, and a set of input and output resources. The complete catalog of processes is defined in Chapter 5 (“Processes”) of the XJDF Specification.

Having these two distinct views allows implementers to write either a pure product description (without requiring any manufacturing knowledge), detailed production instructions for an executing device, or a combination of both—making XJDF adaptable to a wide range of integration scenarios.


## Gray Boxes
*Gray Box* is a foundational concept in XJDF, carried over from JDF 1.x to describe an abstraction level between a Black Box and a White Box. By definition, a Black Box is a system or device whose internal workings are hidden and where only the external inputs and outputs are known. Conversely, a White Box is a system whose internal structure and operations are fully exposed. A Gray Box sits between the two.

Because XJDF-enabled devices and applications typically accept not only input and output specifications but also selected internal process parameters, they are modeled conceptually as Gray Boxes. **Even though most XJDF integrations are effectively Black Box integrations, XJDF does not distinguish between the two: from the perspective of XJDF, every integrated system is a Gray Box, and the corresponding XJDF Document describes it.**

Even a pure product description used for job submission represents a Gray Box. XJDF Documents containing only a product description are primarily used in Web-To-Print environments—for instance, to connect an e-commerce storefront, a print broker platform, or a customer's ERP system to a print shop's MIS in order to automate print procurement. Below is an XJDF stub for a product description Gray Box:

```xml title="Example: Gray Box 'product description'"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="Product"
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

The `@Types` attribute on the root `XJDF` element defines the Gray Box and is one of the first attributes inspected when parsing an XJDF Document. Product description Gray Boxes are identified by `@Types="Product"`. The value "`Product` specifies that the products that are described shall be produced without complete knowledge of the production workflow" [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3 Table 3.1]. When a job is submitted in a Web-To-Print environment, determining the actual production workflow is the responsibility of the MIS. As the central management system for production, the MIS calculates or selects the appropriate sequence of manufacturing steps required to produce the ordered product.

A production workflow defined by the MIS is a sequence of individual processes. A physical production device or software application may execute either a single process or multiple combined processes. For example, a buckle folder executes the single process `Folding`, whereas a modern saddle stitcher may combine `Collecting`, `Stitching`, and `Trimming` to finish a magazine in a single pass. Conceptually, both the folding machine and the saddle stitcher are addressed as Gray Boxes:

<figure>
  <Image src={grayBoxes} alt="Gray Boxes 'Folding' and 'Collecting Stitching Trimming'" />
  <figcaption>Figure: Gray Boxes 'Folding' and 'Collecting Stitching Trimming'</figcaption>
</figure>

Once the production workflow has been planned, the MIS routes the job from one Gray Box to the next, addressing each Gray Box with its own dedicated XJDF Document.

All processes to be executed by the receiving Gray Box must be declared in the `@Types` attribute. As seen above, `@Types` can be set to `"Product"`, a single process name, or a whitespace-separated list of process names. The following XJDF stub addresses a `"Folding"` Gray Box (a folding machine):

```xml title="Example: Gray Box 'Folding'"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="Folding" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

To address a Gray Box that executes a single folding process, `@Types` is set to `"Folding"`. When addressing a multi-step device—such as a saddle stitcher that performs `Collecting`, `Stitching`, and `Trimming`—`@Types` contains a whitespace-separated list of those process names:

```xml title="Example: Gray Box 'Collecting Stitching Trimming'"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="Collecting Stitching Trimming" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

The order of the process names in `@Types` specifies the order in which the processes are to be executed: here, `Collecting` is performed first, followed by `Stitching`, and finally `Trimming`.

It is also valid to combine explicit process names with the `"Product"` keyword:

```xml title="Example: Combination of Gray Box and product description"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="ConventionalPrinting Cutting Product"      
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

Combining process names with `"Product"` indicates that the production workflow is only partially predefined, leaving the remaining steps to be determined downstream.


## Common XML Structure
Let us begin with an abstract XJDF Document that illustrates the common XML structure. Elements that are rarely used in everyday scenarios have been omitted for clarity.

Most examples in this chapter are excerpts from real-world documents. At this stage, the focus is on understanding the overall hierarchy of XML elements in an XJDF Document rather than every domain-specific attribute; many of these examples are revisited in later chapters to examine their semantics in detail. Below is an abstract element tree representing the standard structure of an XJDF Document:

```xml title="Example: Common XJDF Structure"
<XJDF JobID="5" Types="ProcessName_1 ProcessName_2" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    <AuditPool />
    <Comment />
    <GeneralID />
    <ProductList>
        <Product>
            <Intent Name="SpecificIntent">
                <SpecificIntent />
            </Intent>
            <!-- further product intents go here -->

        </Product>
        <!-- further products go here -->

    </ProductList>
    <ResourceSet Name="SpecificResource">
        <Resource>
            <AmountPool />
            <Part />
            <SpecificResource />
        </Resource>
        <!-- further resources of the same type may go here -->

    </ResourceSet>
    <!-- further ResourceSets go here -->

</XJDF>
```

The four core structural elements in an XJDF Document are `Product`, `Intent`, `ResourceSet`, and `Resource`. Virtually all product and process data is organized within these four elements at strictly defined locations in the hierarchy.

The ordering of child elements is prescribed by the specification: “In general the order of elements is lexically sorted in ascending order. Exceptions to alphabetical sorting will be explicitly called out in the relevant sections. [...] Attributes NEED NOT be sorted within an element" [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 1.3.5.1 "Order of Child Elements"].

### XJDF Element
Every XJDF Document begins with the `XJDF` root node, which may appear only once per document:

```xml title="Example: XJDF Element"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="Folding" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

Because an XJDF Document always operates within the context of a specific job and targets a specific Gray Box (or product description), the `@JobID` and `@Types` attributes are mandatory. `@JobID` holds the unique identifier of the job, while `@Types` lists the logical processes covered by the target Gray Box (or `"Product"` for a product description).

The `@xmlns` attribute is a standard XML namespace declaration. “XML namespaces provide a simple method for qualifying element and attribute names used in Extensible Markup Language documents by associating them with namespaces identified by URI references” [@xml_namespaces_2009]. The namespace URI for XJDF 2.0 documents is `http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0`, and future major versions of XJDF will define their own versioned namespace URIs.

The `XJDF` root node can contain five primary types of child elements, all of which are optional: `AuditPool` and `ProductList` may each occur at most once per document, whereas `Comment`, `GeneralID`, and `ResourceSet` may occur multiple times.

### AuditPool Element
The `AuditPool` element may occur at most once and serves as a container for the post-facto execution log of a Gray Box. In many setups, the events recorded in the `AuditPool` are also broadcast to the managing system in real time as XJMF messages when they occur (XJMF messaging is covered in detail in Chapter 6).

Entries in the `AuditPool` include notifications and events that occurred during job execution, actual resource consumption, and process state transitions with their start and end timestamps. This makes `AuditPool` the primary element for production data acquisition. "In contrast to most other elements in XJDF the child elements of AuditPool SHALL be ordered chronologically from oldest to newest rather than alphabetically" [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.4.1 "AuditPool"]. `AuditPool` elements are typically included in the XJDF Document returned to the management application upon job completion:

```xml title="Example: AuditPool Element"
[...]
<AuditPool>
    <AuditCreated>
        <Header ID="I42" DeviceID="DEV_ID" Time="2016-12-28T10:45:05Z" />
    </AuditCreated>
    <AuditStatus>
        <Header ID="I43" DeviceID="DEV_ID" Time="2016-12-28T10:47:19Z" />
        <DeviceInfo Status="Production" Speed="6000">
            <JobPhase JobID="Job-42" JobPartID="Front" Status="InProgress"
                StartTime="2016-12-28T10:40:00Z" PercentCompleted="31.66"
                QueueEntryID="I434" Amount="1343" Waste="85"/>
        </DeviceInfo>
    </AuditStatus>
    <AuditNotification>
        <Header ID="I44" DeviceID="DEV_ID" Time="2016-12-28T10:49:26Z" />
        <Notification Class="Information">
            <Event EventID="3092" EventValue="Paper stock is low." />
        </Notification>
    </AuditNotification>
    [...]
</AuditPool>
[...]
```

Every status change, notification, or resource update is appended chronologically to the `AuditPool`. Its child elements closely mirror the structure of XJMF messages; in practice, the `AuditPool` is often an aggregation of the XJMF messages emitted by the device while processing the job.

Common child elements of `AuditPool` include `AuditNotification` and `AuditStatus`. `AuditNotification` “logs individual events that occurred during processing” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3 Table 3.6 "AuditPool/AuditNotification"], whereas `AuditStatus` “logs start and end times of any process states and sub-states” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3 Table 3.6 "AuditPool/AuditStatus"]. The complete list of audit elements is available in the XJDF Specification.

Every specific audit element contains at least a `Header` child element that records metadata such as the creation timestamp (`@Time`) and the identifier of the originating device (`@DeviceID`). The audit payload itself is stored in a sibling element alongside `Header`—for instance, `AuditStatus` contains a `DeviceInfo` element holding the logged status data.

### Comment Element
`Comment` is a general-purpose XJDF element that can appear at several locations in the document hierarchy, most notably as a direct child of the root `XJDF` element. Multiple `Comment` elements may appear at each valid location.

Because comments are free-form text intended for human operators, relying on them always implies manual reading and interpretation during production. From an automation perspective, comments reduce the degree of hands-off automation:

```xml title="Example: Comment Element"
<XJDF ...>
    [...]
    <Comment Type="Instruction" TimeStamp="2016-08-20T14:44:02+02:00" 
            PersonalID="cnorris">
        Please take a special care for the image on the upper right.
        This is the leading motive of the product.
    </Comment>
    <Comment Type="JobDescription" TimeStamp="2016-08-20T14:44:02+02:00" 
           PersonalID="cnorris">
       Flyer A6 
       4c both sides
       135 gsm glossy paper
    </Comment>
    <!-- further comments may go here -->
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

Comment metadata—such as the author (`@PersonalID`), category (`@Type`), and creation time (`@TimeStamp`)—is stored in attributes on the `Comment` element, while the comment text itself is stored as plain text content inside the element.

Comments are strictly for human information and must never be used to drive automated device behavior. Applications and devices should never parse `Comment` text for keywords to trigger presettings or alter processing logic; doing so undermines interoperability and increases long-term maintenance costs.

### GeneralID Element
Like `Comment`, `GeneralID` is a general-purpose element that can be placed at multiple locations in the XJDF hierarchy and may occur multiple times at each location.

“GeneralID describes a generic identifier. The name or usage of the identifier is specified in `@IDUsage`, and the specific value of the variable is specified in `@IDValue`. The data type is specified in `@DataType`” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 8.15 "GeneralID"].

```xml title="Example: GeneralID Element"
[...]
<GeneralID IDUsage="Height" DataType="integer" IDValue="1440"/>
<GeneralID IDUsage="Width" DataType="integer" IDValue="720"/>
[...]
```

The example above defines two `GeneralID` elements representing a height and a width value. Because `GeneralID` acts as a generic key-value pair that can hold almost any custom datum, it is a frequent source of interoperability problems. When designing a new XJDF interface, developers are often tempted to use `GeneralID` to map internal system variables one-to-one without consulting the XJDF data model. While this may feel like a convenient shortcut initially, it severely reduces compatibility with standard third-party applications. Whenever standard XJDF elements do not cover a requirement, extending XJDF via a dedicated foreign namespace is a much cleaner and more sustainable approach (see the section “Extensibility” below).

### ProductList Element
The `ProductList` element may appear at most once per document and contains the process-independent product descriptions. Even when an XJDF Document is sent to a single-purpose shop-floor device, it is good practice to include the complete `ProductList` describing the final product. Providing the product description places the production step in its proper context: while a folding machine technically only needs a `@JobID` and `FoldingParams` to run, showing the full product description on the operator console helps the operator understand the overall product—especially when producing a single component of a complex multi-part job. Giving operators visibility into the final product speeds up make-ready and makes it much easier to spot production anomalies early. Although the XJDF Specification does not mandate a `ProductList` in every process XJDF Document, including one and displaying it to the operator is strongly recommended.

The following snippet shows a `ProductList` containing two independent print products:

```xml title="Example: ProductList Element"
[...]
<ProductList>
    <Product Amount="250" IsRoot="true">
        <Intent Name="ColorIntent">
            <ColorIntent>
                <SurfaceColor ColorsUsed="Cyan Magenta Yellow Black" 
                    Surface="Front"/>
            </ColorIntent>
        </Intent>
        <Intent Name="LayoutIntent">
            <LayoutIntent FinishedDimensions="1683.779 2383.937 0.0" 
                Sides="OneSided"/>
        </Intent>
        <Intent Name="MediaIntent">
            <MediaIntent MediaQuality="135_GLOSS"/>
        </Intent>
    </Product>
    <Product Amount="1000" IsRoot="true">
        <Intent Name="ColorIntent">
            <ColorIntent>
                <SurfaceColor ColorsUsed="Cyan Magenta Yellow Black" 
                    Surface="Front"/>
            </ColorIntent>
        </Intent>
        <Intent Name="LayoutIntent">
            <LayoutIntent FinishedDimensions="841.889 1190.551 0.0" 
                Sides="OneSided"/>
        </Intent>
        <Intent Name="MediaIntent">
            <MediaIntent MediaQuality="135_GLOSS"/>
        </Intent>
    </Product>
</ProductList>
[...]
```

This `ProductList` specifies two distinct products: the first describes 250 DIN A1 posters (594 mm × 841 mm), printed four-color one-sided on 135 gsm glossy paper; the second describes 1,000 DIN A3 posters (297 mm × 420 mm) with the same color and media characteristics. Each `Product` element carries an `@Amount` attribute and a list of `Intent` child elements (`ColorIntent`, `LayoutIntent`, `MediaIntent`, etc.) that define the product's properties. The complete catalog of product intents is documented in Chapter 4 (“Product Intent”) of the XJDF Specification.

Whenever a `ProductList` contains more than one `Product` element, the `//Product/@IsRoot` attribute must be specified. If `@IsRoot="true"`, “this Product element is a self-contained product. If false, this Product element is a child product of another one, such as a cover or insert” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 4 Table 4.1 "Product/IsRoot"]. By combining a root product with child products, complex multi-part products—such as a magazine with a separate cover—can be modeled cleanly (see Chapter 4, “Product Description”).

### ResourceSet Element
Both product descriptions and process descriptions rely on resources. In XJDF, resources are represented by `Resource` elements grouped inside `ResourceSet` containers and describe the physical or logical entities consumed, produced, or referenced in a workflow. Resources belonging to the same logical group—such as the four printing plates of a four-color press run or the artwork files supplied for a print job—are placed as sibling `Resource` elements within a single `ResourceSet`. `ResourceSet` elements are typically associated with a specific process, though they can also supplement a product description.

The following example shows an input `RunList` resource, which is used to reference external content files (such as customer PDF artwork) for prepress processes or product submissions:

```xml title="Example: RunList Resource"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="RunList" Usage="Input" >
    <Resource>
        <RunList>
            <FileSpec URL="http://example.org/artwork/my.pdf"/>
        </RunList>
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

Most XJDF Documents contain multiple `ResourceSet` elements. Each process typically consumes one or more input `ResourceSet` elements (`@Usage="Input"`) and produces a single output `ResourceSet` (`@Usage="Output"`).

### XJDF Example: Folding
To conclude this overview of the common XML structure, let us examine a complete real-world XJDF Document for a folding operation. Folding is one of the most common postpress finishing processes: a folding machine takes flat printed sheets and folding parameters (`FoldingParams`) as inputs and produces folded sheets or signatures (`Component`) as output.

Because a standalone folding machine is a single-purpose device, it is modeled by a Gray Box with a single process, `"Folding"`. (Readers unfamiliar with industrial folding equipment can find videos and diagrams online under the terms *“Buckle Folding Machine”* and *“Knife Folding Machine”*.)

While this example contains several details that are covered in greater depth in later chapters, reviewing a complete XJDF Document early on helps show how all the structural pieces fit together:

```xml title="Example: XJDF Gray Box Folding"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="Folding" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
    <AuditPool>
        <AuditCreated>
            <Header AgentName="MIS" AgentVersion="1.0" 
                    Time="2016-05-30T21:49:47Z" />
        </AuditCreated>
    </AuditPool>
    <ProductList>
        <Product Amount="2500" DescriptiveName="My Folded Leaflet">
            <Intent Name="LayoutIntent">
                <LayoutIntent Dimensions="841.88 595.27" 
                        Sides="TwoSidedHeadToHead" Pages="2"
                        FinishedDimensions="280.69 595.27 0.0"/>
            </Intent>
            <Intent Name="FoldingIntent">
                <FoldingIntent FoldCatalog="F6-2"/>
            </Intent>
            <Intent Name="ColorIntent">
                <ColorIntent>
                    <SurfaceColor Surface="Front"
                            ColorsUsed="Cyan Magenta Yellow Black"/>
                    <SurfaceColor Surface="Back" 
                            ColorsUsed="Cyan Magenta Yellow Black" />
                </ColorIntent>
            </Intent>
            <Intent Name="MediaIntent">
                <MediaIntent MediaQuality="135_MATT"/>
            </Intent>
        </Product>
    </ProductList>
    <ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Input">
        <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_UNFOLDED">
            <Component Dimensions="841.88 595.27 0.42" 
                    MediaRef="MEDIA-ID" />
        </Resource>
    </ResourceSet>
    <ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Output">
        <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_FOLDED">
            <AmountPool>
                <PartAmount Amount="2580" />
            </AmountPool>
            <Component Dimensions="280.69 595.27 1.26" 
                    MediaRef="MEDIA-ID" />
        </Resource>
    </ResourceSet>
    <ResourceSet Name="FoldingParams" Usage="Input">
        <Resource>
            <FoldingParams FoldCatalog="F6-2"/>
        </Resource>
    </ResourceSet>
    <ResourceSet Name="Media">
        <Resource ID="MEDIA-ID">
            <Media MediaType="Paper" Thickness="150" Weight="135" 
                    ISOPaperSubstrate="PS3" />
        </Resource>
    </ResourceSet>
    <ResourceSet Name="NodeInfo" Usage="Input">
        <Resource>
            <NodeInfo Start="2016-08-30T15:00:00Z" />
        </Resource>
    </ResourceSet>
</XJDF>
```

The document starts with the root `XJDF` node, where `@JobID` identifies the job (`"JOB-42"`) and `@Types="Folding"` specifies the target Gray Box. The `AuditPool` records when and by which application (`@AgentName="MIS"`) the document was created.

The `ProductList` contains a single `Product` element describing the ordered product: 2,500 six-panel folded leaflets printed four-color on both sides on 135 gsm matte paper. Each product characteristic is captured in a dedicated `Intent` child element:

* `LayoutIntent` defines the open (unfolded) dimensions (`@Dimensions`) and the closed (folded) dimensions (`@FinishedDimensions`) in desktop publishing points (dtp points; see “Measurements, Units, and Data Types” below). The `@Sides="TwoSidedHeadToHead"` attribute specifies that the front and back sides are oriented head-to-head when the sheet is turned sideways.
* `FoldingIntent` specifies how the leaflet is to be folded by referencing a standard scheme from the XJDF Fold Catalog (`@FoldCatalog="F6-2"`), which denotes a six-panel accordion/zig-zag fold:

<figure>
  <Image src={f62} alt="Sketch of a F6-2 fold" />
  <figcaption>Figure: Sketch of a F6-2 fold</figcaption>
</figure>

* `ColorIntent` specifies that both the front and back surfaces use the four process colors Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, and Black (CMYK).
* `MediaIntent` requests a 135 gsm matte paper stock (`@MediaQuality="135_MATT"`).

While the `Product` element describes the finished product from the customer's perspective, the manufacturing details required by the folding machine are provided in the `ResourceSet` elements—here `Component`, `FoldingParams`, `Media`, and `NodeInfo`:

* **Component:** “Component is used to describe the various versions of semi-finished goods in the press and postpress area, such as a pile of folded sheets that have been collected and are then be joined and trimmed. Nearly every postpress process has a Component resource as an input as well as an output. Typically the first components in the process chain are some printed sheets or ribbons, while the last Component is a book or a brochure” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.18 "Component"]. Here, the folding process consumes flat sheets (`//ResourceSet/@Usage="Input"`) and produces folded leaflets (`//ResourceSet/@Usage="Output"`). The output `Component` also includes an `AmountPool` specifying a planned production quantity of 2,580 folded leaflets (including 80 extra copies for downstream waste/overage beyond the 2,500 ordered by the customer).
* **FoldingParams:** `FoldingParams` “describes the folding parameters, including the sequence of folding steps” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.37 "FoldingParams"] used to set up the folding machine. In this example, it references the `"F6-2"` scheme from the XJDF Fold Catalog.
* **Media:** A `Media` resource “represents the properties of a raw, unexposed printable surface such as sheet, film or plate” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.58 "Media"]. In contrast to the high-level `MediaIntent`, the `Media` resource provides physical substrate parameters required by the machine (such as `@MediaType="Paper"`, `@Thickness="150"`, and `@Weight="135"`). Note that `Media` is neither a direct input nor a direct output of the `Folding` process; instead, it is referenced by both `Component` resources via `@ID` / `@IDRef` (`//Component/@MediaRef="MEDIA-ID"` pointing to `//ResourceSet[@Name='Media']/Resource/@ID="MEDIA-ID"`). Consequently, the `Media` `ResourceSet` has no `@Usage` attribute, and its `Resource` is the only one carrying an internal `@ID`.
* **NodeInfo:** “The NodeInfo Resource contains information about planned scheduling. It allows MIS systems to plan, schedule and invoice Jobs or Job Parts” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.60 "NodeInfo"].


## Resources
“Resource elements are child elements of a ResourceSet and describe the physical or logical entity in the partition context [...]. For instance a `ResourceSet/@Name`="ExposedMedia" can specify a set of printing plates and each child Resource element will describe an individual plate” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.1 "Resource"]. Resources are typically associated with a specific process, though they can also be associated with products. Every `Resource` element belongs to a parent `ResourceSet`, which “describes a set of one or more Resource elements that are logically grouped together” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.3 "ResourceSet"].

The following snippet illustrates the basic XML structure of a `RunList` resource:

```xml title="Example: RunList Resource"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="RunList" Usage="Input" >
    <Resource>
        <RunList>
            <FileSpec URL="http://example.org/artwork/my.pdf"/>
        </RunList>
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

“A RunList defines one or more printable logical documents or document sets that MAY be defined in one or more external [...] files” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.73 "RunList"]. `RunList` resources are widely used in prepress processes and Web-To-Print job submissions to reference customer artwork or other digital content files via a `FileSpec` child element and its `@URL` attribute.

In the XML hierarchy, `RunList` is a child of `Resource`, which in turn is a child of `ResourceSet`. Even when only a single resource instance is needed, this three-level wrapper structure (`ResourceSet` → `Resource` → specific resource element) must always be written. While this might look verbose at first glance, maintaining a uniform, predictable container hierarchy is essential for the consistency and long-term extensibility of the format: simple use cases remain straightforward, while complex multi-part or partitioned scenarios can be expressed simply by adding sibling `Resource` elements and attributes without changing the XML schema structure.

The `//ResourceSet/@Name` attribute is mandatory on every `ResourceSet` and specifies “the name of the explicit resource that this ResourceSet represents. Child resource elements of this ResourceSet SHALL NOT contain resources that do not match `@Name`” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3 Table 3.4: ResourceSet/@Name].

The `//ResourceSet/@Usage` attribute defines whether the `ResourceSet` is consumed (`"Input"`) or produced (`"Output"`) by the Gray Box. When a `ResourceSet` is neither a direct input nor an output of a process—but instead provides supplementary details referenced by another resource (such as the `Media` resource in the folding example above)—the `//ResourceSet/@Usage` attribute is omitted:

```xml title="Example: Extract Folding Example - References of Resources"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Input">
    <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_UNFOLDED">
        <Component Dimensions="841.88 595.27 0.42" 
            MediaRef="MEDIA-ID" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
<ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Output">
    <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_FOLDED">
        <Component Dimensions="280.69 595.27 1.26"
            MediaRef="MEDIA-ID" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="Media">
    <Resource ID="MEDIA-ID">
        <Media MediaType="Paper" Thickness="150" Weight="135" 
            ISOPaperSubstrate="PS3" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

In this excerpt, the first two `ResourceSet` elements represent the input and output `Component` resources of the folding process (indicated by `//ResourceSet/@Usage`), whereas the third `ResourceSet` (`@Name="Media"`) omits `@Usage` because it is linked from the two `Component` elements via `//Component/@MediaRef="MEDIA-ID"`. Note that the target identifier (`@ID="MEDIA-ID"`) is placed on the generic `Resource` wrapper element (`//Resource/@ID`) rather than on the inner `Media` element.

### Partitioning of Resources
When a `ResourceSet` contains a logical group of multiple `Resource` elements, XJDF uses **partitioning** to distinguish the individual resources and define the context in which each one applies. The following example shows a `RunList` for customer artwork supplied as two separate PDF files—one for the front side and one for the back side:

```xml title="Example: RunList Resource Partitioned by Side"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="RunList">
    <Resource>
        <Part Side="Front" />
        <RunList>
            <FileSpec URL="http://example.org/files/my-page-1.pdf"/>
        </RunList>
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Side="Back" />
        <RunList>
            <FileSpec URL="http://example.org/files/my-page-2.pdf"/>
        </RunList>
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

Alongside the `RunList` element, each `Resource` element contains a `Part` child element. `Part` is the core element used to partition resources: “The Part elements identify the partition context of the Resource element” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.1: Resource/Part]. Here, the `RunList` resources are partitioned by `@Side`, where `//Part/@Side` “denotes the side of the Sheet” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.6: Part/Side]. The first `Resource` references the front-side PDF (`//Part/@Side="Front"`), while the second references the back-side PDF (`//Part/@Side="Back"`). Because child elements in `Resource` follow lexical ordering, `Part` (or `AmountPool`, when present) appears before the specific resource element.

Partitioning is an XJDF-specific mechanism: “Part elements define the context in which the individual Resource is used. Resource partitions are uniquely identified by the Resource/Part elements” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.1.5 "Part"]. In XJDF, only resources can be partitioned. Because the attributes of the `Part` element identify the individual partitions, they are called **Partition Keys**. Common partition keys include `@Side`, `@ProductPart`, `@Separation`, `@BinderySignatureID`, and `@PartVersion`:

* `@ProductPart` “references the `//Product/@ID` that this Part applies to” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.6: Part/ProductPart].
* `@Separation` “identifies a color separation” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.6: Part/Separation] such as `"Cyan"`, `"Magenta"`, `"Yellow"`, or `"Black"`.
* `@PartVersion` is a “Version identifier (e.g., the language version of a catalog)” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.6: Part/ProductVersion].

The complete list of valid partition keys is defined in Section 6.1.5 (“Part”) of the XJDF Specification.

The following example uses the `@Separation` partition key to describe the set of four printing plates required for a four-color offset press run. Although each plate corresponds to a different color separation (`Cyan`, `Magenta`, `Yellow`, and `Black`), they form a single logical group and are therefore represented as four `Resource` elements inside a single `ExposedMedia` `ResourceSet`:

```xml title="Example: Extract of ResourceSets of a ConventionalPrinting XJDF"
<XJDF JobID="JOB-42" Types="ConventionalPrinting"
      xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0">
   [...]
   <ResourceSet Name="ExposedMedia" Usage="Input" ProcessUsage="Plate">
       <Resource>
           <Part Separation="Cyan"/>
           <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
       </Resource>
       <Resource>
           <Part Separation="Magenta"/>
           <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
       </Resource>
       <Resource>
           <Part Separation="Yellow"/>
           <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
       </Resource>
       <Resource>
           <Part Separation="Black"/>
           <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
       </Resource>
   </ResourceSet>
   [...]
</XJDF>
```

A `Part` element may also combine multiple partition keys at once, and a single `Resource` element may contain multiple `Part` child elements. “If multiple Part elements are specified within one Resource, the Resource specifies one entity that applies to both parts” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.1.5 "Part"]. The following example of a bilingual print job illustrates both techniques:

```xml title="Example: Multiple Part elements per Resource"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="ExposedMedia">
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Cyan" PartVersion="German" />
        <Part Separation="Cyan" PartVersion="English" />
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Magenta" PartVersion="German" />
        <Part Separation="Magenta" PartVersion="English" />
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Yellow" PartVersion="German" />
        <Part Separation="Yellow" PartVersion="English" />
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Black" PartVersion="German" />
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Black" PartVersion="English" />
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>

<ResourceSet Name="Media">
    <Resource ID="MEDIA-PLATE-ID">
        <Media MediaType="Plate" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

This example describes five printing plates (`ExposedMedia`) for a job produced in two language versions (`"German"` and `"English"`). Because all text changes are confined to the black channel, the `Cyan`, `Magenta`, and `Yellow` plates are identical across both language runs; each of those three `Resource` elements therefore contains two `Part` elements indicating that the same physical plate is shared by both `"German"` and `"English"`. Only the `Black` separation requires two separate plates—one for `"German"` and one for `"English"`. By sharing the three color plates across both runs, the printer saves three plates and the associated plate-change make-ready time.


## Extensibility
The XJDF Specification provides several officially supported mechanisms for extending the format. However, custom extensions must never duplicate functionality that already exists in standard XJDF. Because XJDF is the result of decades of industry-wide standardization across vendors, consultants, and print service providers, implementers should always use standard elements and attributes wherever possible. While introducing proprietary tags might appear to be a quick shortcut during initial development, custom extensions undermine cross-vendor interoperability and long-term interface stability.

**Whenever a required feature appears to be missing from the standard, the recommended approach is to open an issue in the public CIP4 JIRA issue tracker (see Chapter 8, “Authoring Process”). Discussing the requirement with the CIP4 community will either reveal how to model it using existing standard constructs or lead to an official extension in the next revision of the XJDF Specification. During the interim period, a private foreign namespace can be used.**

“Attributes in a foreign namespace MAY be added to any XJDF element. Elements in a foreign namespace SHALL NOT be specified in XJDF elements other than Notification, Resource and Intent elements” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.5.1 "Foreign Namespaces"]. Specifically, XJDF supports four extension mechanisms:

* Custom intents and resources
* Custom processes
* Custom values in `NMTOKEN` lists
* Custom notifications

When introducing a private namespace, a company should define a single canonical namespace URI and a single prefix, and keep both constant across all its interfaces. “Elements in a foreign namespace SHALL be specified after any elements in the XJDF namespace” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.5.1 "Foreign Namespaces"].

### Custom Intents and Resources
XJDF allows vendors to introduce custom intents and custom resources under a foreign namespace. The following example shows how to define a custom resource:

```xml title="Example: Custom Resources"
<XJDF Types="ProcessName" JobID="5" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0"
        xmlns:foo="http://example.org/foo">
    [...]
    <ResourceSet Name="foo:bar">
        <Resource>
            <foo:bar attribute="myAttributeValue" />
        </Resource>
    </ResourceSet>
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

First, the foreign namespace (`xmlns:foo="http://example.org/foo"`) is declared on the root `XJDF` element. The custom resource element (`<foo:bar>`) is then placed inside a standard `Resource` wrapper, and its full qualified name (including the namespace prefix, `"foo:bar"`) is specified in `//ResourceSet/@Name`.

Defining a custom intent follows the exact same pattern inside a `Product` element:

```xml title="Example: Custom Intents"
<XJDF Types="ProcessName" JobID="5" 
        xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0"
        xmlns:foo="http://example.org/foo">
    [...]
    <ProductList>
        <Product>
            <Intent Name="foo:bar">
                <foo:bar attribute="myAttributeValue" />
            </Intent>
        </Product>
    </ProductList>
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

### Custom Processes
Process types can also be extended: “XJDF defines a basic set of process types. However, because XJDF allows flexible encoding, this list, by definition, will not be complete. Vendors that have specific processes that do not fit in the general XJDF processes and that are not combinations of individual XJDF processes [...] can create process XJDF of their own type” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.5.5 "Extending Process Types"].

To use a custom process, declare the company's foreign namespace URI and prefix on the root `XJDF` element and include the namespace-prefixed process name (separated by a colon, e.g., `"foo:MyCustomProcess"`) in the `@Types` attribute.

### Custom Values in NMTOKEN Lists
“Many Resources contain attributes of type NMTOKEN and some of these have a set of predefined, suggested enumerative values. These lists MAY be extended with private keywords” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.5.4 "Extending NMTOKEN Lists"].

```xml title="Example: Custom NMTOKEN Values"
<XJDF Types="ProcessName" JobID="5"
    xmlns="http://www.CIP4.org/JDFSchema_2_0" >
    [...]
    <ProductList>
        <Product Amount="250">
            <Intent Name="FoldingIntent">
                <FoldingIntent FoldCatalog="F6-MY-FOLD" />
            </Intent>
            [...]
        </Product>
    </ProductList>
    [...]
</XJDF>
```

Because `@FoldCatalog` is of type `NMTOKEN`, its predefined catalog of fold schemes can be extended with custom tokens (such as `"F6-MY-FOLD"` above). Extending an `NMTOKEN` attribute with a custom keyword does not require declaring a foreign XML namespace.

### Custom Notifications
`Notification` elements can likewise be extended with custom child elements in a foreign namespace; refer to the XJDF Specification for details.


## Identifiers
The XJDF Specification defines several types of identifiers (IDs) to reference resources, jobs, worksteps, and devices. Every identifier must be unique within its designated scope—for example, a scope of *Production* means the identifier must be unique across the entire production environment (which may span multiple plants or sites), whereas a scope of *Document* means the identifier is valid only within a single XML file. The table below summarizes the most important identifiers in XJDF:

| ID Name | Description | Scope | Example |
| ------- | ----------- | ----- | ------- |
| `ExternalID` | Unique identifier of physical resources or inventory items | Production | `COMP-34` |
| `ID` / `IDRef` | Internal element identifier and cross-reference within a single XJDF Document | Document | `j3hduw83` |
| `Partition Key` | Identifier of a specific resource partition (attributes on `Part`) | Production | `SHEET-1` |
| `JobID` | Unique identifier of a job | Production | `JB-124` |
| `JobPartID` | Identifier for one or more worksteps of the same type within a job | Production | `Front` |
| `DeviceID` | Unique identifier of a physical device or controller | Production | `PRESS-1` |

### ExternalID
The `@ExternalID` attribute is “an ID of the resource as defined in the MIS system. For instance item codes or article numbers or identifiers on semi-finished products or Resource elements. `@ExternalID` shall be used to uniquely identify resources and products for the purpose of inventory tracking” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.1: Resource/ExternalID]. Any resource can be assigned an item code, SKU, or pallet identifier via `@ExternalID`. An `@ExternalID` must be unique across the entire production system and should remain constant throughout the resource's lifecycle:

```xml title="Example: ExternalID in Resources"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Input">
    <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_UNFOLDED">
        <Component Dimensions="841.88 595.27 0.42"
            MediaRef="MEDIA-ID"/>
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
<ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Output">
    <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_FOLDED">
        <Component Dimensions="280.69 595.27 1.26"
            MediaRef="MEDIA-ID"/>
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

In this excerpt from the folding example, two `Component` resources represent semi-finished goods: the input pile of unfolded sheets (`@ExternalID="COMP_ID_UNFOLDED"`) and the output pile of folded leaflets (`@ExternalID="COMP_ID_FOLDED"`). During the folding operation, the lifecycle of `COMP_ID_UNFOLDED` ends as the flat sheets are consumed, while the lifecycle of `COMP_ID_FOLDED` begins. In a print shop, the `@ExternalID` is typically printed as a barcode on a pallet flag attached to the pile so operators and warehouse systems can identify and track each component.

### ID / IDRef
`@ID` and `@IDRef` attributes are used strictly for internal cross-references within a single XML document. Their values are transient, may change from one transaction to the next, and must never be used as persistent identifiers across a production workflow. In XJDF, `@ID` attributes are placed on `//ResourceSet/@ID`, `//Resource/@ID`, or `//Product/@ID`. “Unless otherwise specified, an `@IDREF` or `@IDREFS` will refer to an individual Resource rather than an entire ResourceSet” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3.3 "ResourceSet"].

```xml title="Example: Internal document reference using ID / IDRef"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Output">
    <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_FOLDED">
        <Component Dimensions="280.69 595.27 1.26" 
            MediaRef="MEDIA-ID" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="Media">
    <Resource ID="MEDIA-ID">
        <Media MediaType="Paper" Thickness="150" Weight="135" 
            ISOPaperSubstrate="PS3" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

Here, the `Component` resource references the `Media` resource via `//Component/@MediaRef="MEDIA-ID"`, which matches `//ResourceSet[@Name='Media']/Resource/@ID`. Note that `"MEDIA-ID"` is *not* the warehouse SKU of the paper stock—it is merely a local XML pointer within this document. A persistent warehouse paper SKU would be stored in `//ResourceSet[@Name='Media']/Resource/@ExternalID`.

### Partition Key
Whenever a `ResourceSet` contains multiple `Resource` elements that are not targeted individually via `@ID` / `@IDRef`, individual resources are selected using **Partition Keys**—the attributes of the `Part` element (see “Partitioning of Resources” above). Unlike `@ExternalID` or `@ID` / `@IDRef`, the values of many partition keys are standardized by the specification:

```xml title="Example: Selection by Partition Keys"
[...]
<ResourceSet Name="ExposedMedia" Usage="Input" ProcessUsage="Plate">
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Cyan"/>
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Magenta"/>
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Yellow"/>
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part Separation="Black"/>
        <ExposedMedia MediaRef="MEDIA-PLATE-ID" PlateType="Exposed" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

In this `ExposedMedia` `ResourceSet`, the four printing plates are distinguished by their `@Separation` partition key (`"Cyan"`, `"Magenta"`, `"Yellow"`, and `"Black"`), as defined in the XJDF Specification:

<figure>
  <Image src={screenPartSeparation} alt="The Definition of the Separation Partition Key (XJDF Spec Extract)" />
  <figcaption>Figure: The Definition of the Separation Partition Key (XJDF Spec Extract)</figcaption>
</figure>

Because `@Separation` has the data type `NMTOKEN`, it is an extensible enumeration: the specification predefines standard values for common process and special separations, while allowing custom spot-color names to be used as well (see “Extensibility” above).

### JobID, JobPartID, DeviceID...
Beyond resource identifiers, XJDF defines numerous specialized identifiers, three of the most frequently used being `@JobID`, `@JobPartID`, and `@DeviceID`:

* **`@JobID`:** The “Job identification used by the application that created the XJDF Job. Typically, a Job is identified by the internal order number of the MIS system that created the Job” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3 Table 3.1: XJDF/JobID] (e.g., `"674R834"`). A `@JobID` must be unique across the production workflow and remain unchanged for the life of the job.
* **`@JobPartID`:** The “JobPartID SHALL identify one or more worksteps of the same type that can be described as one XJDF. `@JobPartID` is internal to the MIS system that created the XJDF” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 3 Table 3.1: XJDF/JobPartID] (e.g., `"Front"` or `"Back"`).
* **`@DeviceID`:** The “identifier of the Device. The `@DeviceID` SHALL be unique within the workflow. `@DeviceID` SHALL be the same over time for a specific Device instance (i.e., SHALL survive reboots)” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6 Table 6.58: Device/DeviceID] (e.g., `"SM-162-8"`).

For every identifier, the XJDF Specification defines its exact semantic purpose, data type, and uniqueness scope. Note that in the specification, “The word ‘Unique’ without further scope details means ‘Unique within the job, message or file depending on the context’” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 1 Table 1.4: Glossay (Unique)].


## Measurements, Units, and Data Types
“XJDF specifies most values in default units. That means that an implementation SHALL use the defined default units and SHALL NOT use alternate units [...]. Processors SHOULD NOT specify a unit unless no default exists, such as when new resources are defined. Then the units SHALL be based on metric units. Overriding the default units that are defined [...] is non-standard and MAY lead to undefined behavior. Any exceptions are specified in the appropriate descriptive tables” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 1.9.1 "Units"]. All default units and their representations are listed in Section 1.9.1 (“Units”) of the XJDF Specification.

The most common physical measurement in XJDF is length. With the exception of microscopic measurements (such as paper thickness in microns), all lengths—including width, height, dimensions, and coordinates—are expressed in **desktop publishing points (dtp points)**, where 1 point equals 1/72 of an inch. Converting between millimeters (`mm`) and dtp points (`dtp`) is straightforward:

```java title="Conversion dtp points (dtp) to millimeter (mm) and vice versa"
// Conversion dtp points (dtp) to millimeter (mm)
mm = dtp * 25.4 / 72.0

// Conversion millimeter (mm) to dtp points (dtp)
dtp = mm * 72.0 / 25.4
```

Depending on whether an attribute describes a single distance, a 2D area, a bounding box, or a 3D volume, length values are encoded using one of several standard XJDF data types:

| Data Type | Description | Example |
| --------- | ----------- | ------- |
| `float` | A single floating-point number used for scalar distances such as width, height, or bleed. | `"595.27"` |
| `rectangle` | Four whitespace-separated floats (`llx lly urx ury`) specifying the lower-left X, lower-left Y, upper-right X, and upper-right Y coordinates of a rectangular area on a page, sheet, or surface. The coordinate origin `(0, 0)` is always the lower-left corner of the parent object. | `"10.0 10.0 290.69 605.27"` |
| `shape` | Three non-negative floats (`x y z`) specifying the width (`x`), height (`y`), and depth/thickness (`z`) of a three-dimensional object such as a folded component or book block. | `"280.69 595.27 1.26"` |
| `XYPair` | Two whitespace-separated floats (`x y`) used for two-dimensional sizes (such as unfolded sheet `@Dimensions`) or 2D `(x, y)` coordinates on a surface. | `"290.69 605.27"` |

The following excerpt from the folding example illustrates how these length data types appear in practice:

```xml title="Example: Common data types which are relevant in combination with lengths"
[...]
<ProductList>
    <Product Amount="2500" DescriptiveName="My Folded Leaflet">
        <Intent Name="LayoutIntent">
            <LayoutIntent Dimensions="841.88 595.27"
                    Sides="TwoSidedHeadToHead" Pages="2"
                    FinishedDimensions="280.69 595.27 0.0"/>
        </Intent>
        [...]
    </Product>
</ProductList>
<ResourceSet Name="Component" Usage="Input">
    <Resource ExternalID="COMP_ID_UNFOLDED">
        <Component Dimensions="841.88 595.27 0.42"
                MediaRef="MEDIA-ID" />
    </Resource>
</ResourceSet>
[...]
```

This snippet contains three length-related attributes: `@Dimensions` and `@FinishedDimensions` on `LayoutIntent`, and `@Dimensions` on `Component`. `//LayoutIntent/@Dimensions` has the data type `XYPair` (2D open sheet size), whereas `//LayoutIntent/@FinishedDimensions` and `//Component/@Dimensions` have the data type `shape` (3D width, height, and thickness). The figure below shows the corresponding attribute definitions for `LayoutIntent` in the XJDF Specification:

  <figcaption>Figure: Excerpt of the LayoutIntent definition in the XJDF Specification</figcaption>

## Folding Schemes and Pagination
Folding and pagination are fundamental concepts in print production. **Folding** defines how a flat printed sheet is folded into a signature or finished product (e.g., an accordion/zig-zag fold), while **pagination** defines how individual document pages are distributed, positioned, and oriented across the press sheet so that they appear in the right sequence and orientation after folding and binding.

In XJDF, every print product is modeled as one or more product parts. A simple flyer or folded leaflet consists of a single product part, whereas a complex product comprises two or more parts. For example, the diagram below shows a 24-page softcover brochure printed on two different paper stocks: a 4-page `"Cover"` printed on heavier premium stock and a 20-page `"Body"` printed on standard text paper:

<figure>
  <Image src={diagramBrochureProductParts} alt="Product parts of a softcover-brochure" />
  <figcaption>Figure: Product parts of a softcover-brochure</figcaption>
</figure>

To manufacture the product, each product part is divided into one or more **bindery signatures**. “A BinderySignature represents both sides of a folding signature, a die cut surface or a flat product such as a postcard, each with one or more pages” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.6 "BinderySignature"]. Each bindery signature is characterized by a specific folding scheme. How a product part is split into bindery signatures depends on the available press and finishing equipment and the chosen production plan.

> **Note:** A `BinderySignature` is purely the logical description of the fold pattern and page arrangement. In the physical production workflow, each logical bindery signature corresponds to a physical `Component` resource representing “the various versions of semi-finished goods in the press and postpress area, such as a pile of folded sheets that have been collected and are then be joined and trimmed” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 6.18 "Component"].

For instance, a 16-page single-part brochure could be produced as one 16-page bindery signature, two 8-page bindery signatures, four 4-page bindery signatures, or a combination thereof. The MIS (or a production planner) determines the most cost-effective signature breakdown.

In our 24-page brochure example, the job is divided into three bindery signatures: a 4-page signature for the cover, plus a 16-page signature and a 4-page signature for the 20-page body. The exact folding scheme of each signature is specified via its `@FoldCatalog` identifier:

Pagination determines “the distribution and orientation of pages on a BinderySignature [...]" [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 5.4.22.1 "Pagination in Stripping"] and depends directly on both the folding schemes of the individual signatures and the binding method used to assemble them (**collecting** vs. **gathering**):

* **Collecting (Inset binding):** Signatures are nested inside one another along a saddle/spine, with the first signature on the outside and subsequent signatures placed inside it (as in our saddle-stitched brochure example above).
* **Gathering (Collating):** Signatures are stacked sequentially on top of one another so that page numbers run consecutively from the first signature at the top of the stack to the last signature at the bottom (used for perfect binding, hardcover books, and pads).

Conceptually, a bindery signature is the fundamental building block into which every print product is decomposed—even a flat, unfolded two-sided flyer is represented as an `"F2-1"` bindery signature. At minimum, a `BinderySignature` resource defines a `@FoldCatalog` attribute and is identified by a `@BinderySignatureID` partition key:

```xml title="Example: A BinderySignature ResourceSet"

<ResourceSet Name="BinderySignature" Usage="Input">
    <Resource>
        <Part BinderySignatureID="BS-1" ProductPart="COVER-ID" />
        <BinderySignature FoldCatalog="F4-1"/>
    </Resource>
    <Resource>
        <Part BinderySignatureID="BS-2" ProductPart="BODY-ID" />
```

In this notation, an upward arrow (`⬆`) indicates a "Fold Up" (folding the left/bottom section *over* the right/top section), while a downward arrow (`⬇`) indicates a "Fold Down" (folding the left/bottom section *under* the right/top section). While the XJDF Fold Catalog covers all standard commercial folding schemes, XJDF also supports generic fold identifiers (such as `"F18-X"`) as well as custom `NMTOKEN` fold names (see “Extensibility” above) when a non-standard fold is required—though custom or generic schemes typically require manual machine setup.

### XJDF Example: Collecting
Modern saddle stitchers not only collect pre-folded signatures along a saddle chain, but also stitch the spine and trim the three open edges in a single continuous pass—consuming pre-folded signature components as input and delivering finished brochures as output.

In XJDF, such a machine is modeled as a Gray Box combining the three processes `Collecting`, `Stitching`, and `Trimming`:

* **`Collecting`** “collects folded sheets or partial products[...]. The first component to enter the workflow lies at the bottom of the pile collected on a saddle, and the sequence of the input components that follows depends upon the produced component” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 5.6.7 "Collecting"].
* **`Stitching`** defines how “gathered or collected sheets or signatures are stitched together” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 5.6.33 "Stitching"].
* **`Trimming`** “is performed to adjust a book block or sheet to its final size” [@xjdf_ip1, chap. 5.6.37 "Trimming"] by trimming the head, foot, and face edges.

Below is the complete XJDF Document for our 24-page softcover magazine on a `"Collecting Stitching Trimming"` Gray Box:

While the overall structure mirrors the earlier folding example, notice a few key additions:

1. **Complex Product in `ProductList`:** Instead of a single `Product`, the `ProductList` models a multi-part magazine consisting of a root product (`@IsRoot="true"`) and two child products (`@ID="COVER-ID"` and `@ID="BODY-ID"`, both with `@IsRoot="false"`). Complex product descriptions are covered in detail in the next chapter.
2. **`BinderySignature` and `Assembly`:** The 24-page magazine is split into three bindery signatures (`"BS-1"` for the 4-page cover, `"BS-2"` for the 16-page body signature, and `"BS-3"` for the 4-page center signature), each linked to its respective child product via the `@ProductPart` partition key. The `Assembly` resource specifies the order (`@BinderySignatureIDs="BS-1 BS-2 BS-3"`) and method (`@Order="Collecting"`) in which the signatures are nested together—with `"BS-1"` on the outside and `"BS-2"` and `"BS-3"` inset within one another.
3. **Physical `Component` Partitions:** Each logical `BinderySignature` corresponds to an untrimmed physical input `Component` linked via the `@BinderySignatureID` partition key and carrying its own warehouse pallet identifier (`@ExternalID="COMP-BS-1"`, etc.).
4. **`StitchingParams` and `TrimmingParams`:** Provide the process parameters for the `Stitching` step (saddle-stitching with eyelet staples) and the `Trimming` step (trimming the finished magazine from `852.68 × 603.67` dtp points down to its final A4 size of `841.88 × 595.27` dtp points).
